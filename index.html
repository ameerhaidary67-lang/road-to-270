Under the hood

How the model thinks

This isn't a lookup table. It mirrors how professional forecasters build an election forecast — Abramowitz's "Time for Change," Hibbs's "Bread and Peace," and the simulation approach of outlets like 538 and The Economist — in four layers, every one of them transparent.

LAYER 01

Fundamentals → national vote

The economy and presidential approval set an expected national popular-vote margin for the party in power (Republicans, who won in 2024) — before any state is drawn.

LAYER 02

Demographic electorate

Each state is a stack of five voter blocks — non-college white, college white, Black, Latino, Asian/other — each with a real population share, turnout, and partisan lean. Candidate appeal and turnout shifts act through these blocks.

LAYER 03

Calibration to 2024

Every state is anchored to its real 2024 result, so the model predicts changes from reality and never drifts into fantasy maps.

LAYER 04

Monte Carlo uncertainty

2,001 simulated elections add correlated national error plus state-level noise, producing win probabilities, a range of outcomes, and the tipping-point state.

National margin (GOP − Dem) = 1.5 + 0.30·approval + 0.9·(GDP−2.2) − 0.8·(unemp−4.2) − 0.7·(inflation−2.5)
  + 0.9·(real income−1.8) + 0.5·(real wages−0.5) + 0.04·(sentiment−75) + 0.06·(right-track+20)
  − 0.5·generic ballot − 1.5·(thermostatic−0.5) − 0.4·(party-ID−2) − abortion + 0.8·crisis + Oct. surprise + 0.4·ad edge
  + 0.10·(favorabilityR−favorabilityD) − 1.5·(|ideologyR|−|ideologyD|)

State margin = margin2024 + elasticity·(national shift) + demographic & turnout Δ + candidate-appeal Δ + home-state bonus

Glossary

Two-party margin
The gap between the two major parties, ignoring third parties. "R+3" means the Republican leads by 3 points.
Elasticity
How much a state swings when the national mood shifts. Elastic states (≈1.3) move a lot; inelastic ones (≈0.6) barely budge.
Tipping-point state
The state that provides the decisive 270th electoral vote when you line states up from most to least favorable. Whoever wins it wins the election.
Monte Carlo
Running the election thousands of times with random, realistic error to see the range of outcomes, not one guess.
Credible interval
The "80% range" means 8 in 10 simulations landed between those two electoral-vote totals.
Fundamentals
The non-campaign basics — the economy, approval, incumbency — that political scientists show predict most of the result.

Assumptions & honest limitations

  • No 2028 polling exists yet — every number is a modeled estimate, not a measurement.
  • Coefficients are illustrative, chosen to reproduce historical relationships; they are not fitted regression weights.
  • Demographic shares approximate the 2024 electorate; candidate traits are author estimates of public figures.
  • Maine and Nebraska are shown winner-take-all for clarity (they actually split by district).
  • The simulation's error size is an assumption; real forecasting error varies by how far out you are.

Data sources: U.S. Census ACS (demographics), certified state results (2024 baseline & 1960–2024 history), economic indicators patterned on BEA/BLS/University of Michigan series. See Data & sources.
